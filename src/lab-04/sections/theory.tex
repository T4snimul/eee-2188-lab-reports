\section{Theory}
\markright{Theory}

A rectifier is an electronic circuit that converts alternating current (AC) into pulsating direct current (DC). Diodes are fundamental components used in rectification because they only permit current to flow in one direction when forward-biased and block current when reverse-biased \cite{lab04_mehta2012}. While a single diode can accomplish half-wave rectification, allowing only one half-cycle of the AC signal to pass through, a full-wave rectifier utilizes multiple diodes to convert both positive and negative half-cycles of the input AC signal into DC output \cite{lab04_boylestad2013}.

\subsection{Full-Wave Rectifier Configurations}

There are two primary types of full-wave rectifiers:

\paragraph{Center-Tapped Transformer Rectifier:}
This configuration uses a center-tapped transformer whose secondary winding is divided into two equal halves. Two silicon diodes are connected to the transformer ends, with the load resistor $R_L$ positioned between the diodes and the center tap. During the positive half-cycle of the AC input, the upper diode conducts while the lower diode is reverse-biased. During the negative half-cycle, this condition reverses, and the lower diode conducts. This arrangement ensures that current flows through the load resistor in the same direction during both half-cycles, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:center_tapped_circuit}.

\paragraph{Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier:}
The bridge rectifier configuration, shown in Figure \ref{fig:bridge_circuit}, uses four diodes arranged in a diamond configuration around the load resistor. During the positive half-cycle, two diodes (opposite corners) conduct simultaneously, while during the negative half-cycle, the other two diodes conduct. This configuration provides full-wave rectification without requiring a center-tapped transformer.

\subsection{Comparison of Full-Wave and Half-Wave Rectification}

The full-wave rectifier offers several significant advantages over the half-wave rectifier \cite{lab04_boylestad2013}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
        \setlength{\parskip}{2pt}
        \setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Higher average DC output:} The full-wave rectifier produces twice the average DC voltage compared to a half-wave rectifier for the same input amplitude
  \item \textbf{Reduced ripple:} The output waveform is smoother because the output pulses occur at twice the frequency of the input
  \item \textbf{Reduced power loss:} More efficient power conversion due to utilization of both half-cycles
  \item \textbf{Better filtering:} Easier to filter and reduce ripple using smaller capacitors
\end{enumerate}

However, the center-tapped configuration has drawbacks: it requires a center-tapped transformer (which is bulkier and more expensive) and takes up additional space. The bridge rectifier eliminates the need for a center-tapped transformer, making it more compact and cost-effective.

\subsection{Filter Capacitor and Output Smoothing}

When a capacitor is connected across the load resistor, it charges during the conducting phase of the diodes and discharges through the load during the non-conducting phase. This filtering action significantly reduces the ripple voltage in the output, providing a more stable DC output. However, the capacitor creates a higher peak current demand, which can stress the diodes. The percent loss is calculated using Eq. \eqref{eq:percent_loss}:

\begin{equation}
  \% \text{ of loss} = \frac{V_{\text{in}} - V_{\text{out}}}{V_{\text{in}}} \times 100\%
  \label{eq:percent_loss}
\end{equation}

The rectification process depends on the forward voltage drop across the diodes (approximately $0.6\text{--}0.7\,\text{V}$ for silicon diodes) and the characteristics of the input signal. Different input waveforms (sinusoidal, triangular, or square) produce different peak voltages at the output, thus affecting the rectification efficiency and voltage loss \cite{lab04_mehta2012}.
