\subsection*{Background and Historical Context}

The discipline of electronics traces its origin to the late nineteenth century, when
physicists first began studying the behavior of electric charge in vacuum. The invention
of the thermionic (vacuum) diode by Sir John Ambrose Fleming in 1904, and the triode by
Lee de Forest in 1906, marked the dawn of active electronic devices capable of amplifying
signals \cite{horowitz2015}. For the following four decades, vacuum tubes powered radio
receivers, audio amplifiers, radar systems, and the earliest electronic computers. The
ENIAC computer (1945), for instance, contained 17,468 vacuum tubes and consumed roughly
150 kW of electrical power.

The pivotal transformation came in 1947, when William Shockley, John Bardeen, and Walter
Brattain at Bell Laboratories demonstrated the first transistor---a compact, solid-state
device built from germanium semiconductor material that could amplify current without a
vacuum envelope \cite{razavi2013}. This invention earned the trio the 1956 Nobel Prize in
Physics and laid the foundation for every modern electronic system. Silicon gradually
replaced germanium through the 1950s, and in 1958--1959, Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments)
and Robert Noyce (Fairchild Semiconductor) independently developed the integrated circuit
(IC), placing multiple transistors and passive components on a single semiconductor die.
Gordon Moore's 1965 observation---that the number of transistors on an IC doubles
approximately every two years---has held remarkably true for over five decades, enabling
the billions of transistors found on contemporary microprocessors \cite{sedra2020}.

Today, electronics is the enabling technology behind communication networks, medical
devices, power systems, transportation, and computing. The components studied in this
experiment---resistors, capacitors, diodes, transistors, and operational amplifiers---are
the fundamental building blocks from which all of these systems are constructed.

\subsection*{Fundamental Laws Governing Electronic Circuits}

All electronic circuits obey a small set of universal physical laws. Ohm's Law
states that the voltage $V$ across a resistive element is proportional to the current $I$
flowing through it: $V = IR$, where $R$ is the resistance in ohms. In a more general
sense, any two-terminal element is characterized by its voltage--current (V--I)
relationship, which may be linear (resistor), nonlinear (diode, transistor), or
energy-storing (capacitor, inductor) \cite{floyd2012}.

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of all currents
entering any node of a circuit is zero (conservation of charge). Kirchhoff's
Voltage Law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop
is zero (conservation of energy). These two laws, together with the V--I relationships of
the individual components, form a complete system of equations that uniquely determines
the voltages and currents at every point in a circuit \cite{floyd2012}.

Figure~\ref{fig:vi_char} illustrates the contrast between the linear V--I relationship
of a resistor and the strongly nonlinear exponential characteristic of a p--n junction
diode, described by the Shockley equation:
\[
  I_D = I_S \left( e^{V_D / nV_T} - 1 \right)
\]
where $I_S$ is the reverse saturation current, $n$ is the ideality factor
($1 \leq n \leq 2$), and $V_T = kT/q \approx 26\,\text{mV}$ at room temperature is the
thermal voltage.

\subsection*{Active vs.\ Passive Components}

Electronic components are broadly classified into passive and
active types. Passive components---resistors, capacitors, inductors, and
diodes---cannot supply energy to a circuit; they can only dissipate, store, or redirect
it. Active components---transistors (BJT and MOSFET) and operational amplifiers---can
control or amplify signals by drawing power from an external supply, delivering more
energy to the output than they receive at the input \cite{sedra2020}. This distinction is
fundamental: a passive filter can shape a signal but attenuates it; an active filter can
both shape and amplify it.

\subsection*{Measurement Instruments}

Understanding and verifying circuit behavior requires measurement. The two principal
instruments used in this sessional are the multimeter and the
oscilloscope. The multimeter provides accurate DC and AC scalar measurements
(voltage, current, resistance, continuity), while the oscilloscope displays the full
time-domain waveform of a signal, revealing its shape, frequency, amplitude, phase, and
transient behavior \cite{wiki:oscilloscope}. Figure~\ref{fig:scope_wave} shows a
sinusoidal waveform as displayed on a digital storage oscilloscope, with key measurement
parameters indicated. The oscilloscope is indispensable wherever signals change over time
faster than the human eye or a multimeter can track.
